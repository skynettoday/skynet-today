Anthropic listed on the Nasdaq under the ticker ANT on Thursday, Nov. 19, 2026, in the largest initial share sale on record. The company priced 980 million shares at $83 the day before, above the $70-to-$78 range covered in [#347](https://lastweekin.ai/p/347), raising $81.3 billion. An underwriters' option on up to 147 million more Class A shares could take the total to $93.5 billion, topping the $85.7 billion SpaceX raised in June. Anthropic paid $550 million in fees to the banks.

The stock opened at $111.50, 34 percent above the IPO price, briefly implying a fully diluted value near $2 trillion, then faded to close at $97.17, up 17 percent, for a $1.27 trillion valuation at the bell. On Friday it gave back most of that gain, closing about 9 percent lower and only narrowly above the offer price after falling as much as 14 percent during the session. Dario and Daniela Amodei were at the Nasdaq MarketSite in New York for the debut. In his remarks before the opening bell, Dario Amodei warned about AI's risks and called machine intelligence one of the most significant developments in human history.

The listing did not lift the rest of the market, which broke sharply instead. On Thursday the Dow fell about 1,100 points, or 2.19 percent, the Nasdaq Composite lost 2.25 percent, and the Cboe Volatility Index jumped almost 16 percent to nearly 29. Friday deepened it: the Nasdaq fell 5.28 percent to 22,617.73, erasing its gains for 2026, while the S&P 500 lost 3.7 percent and the Dow 1.93 percent to its first close below 50,000 since June. Both indexes ended more than 10 percent below their record closing highs, confirming corrections, and all three posted their steepest weekly losses since April 2025. Chip and AI infrastructure names led the way down: Nvidia fell 13 percent after Reuters reported an SEC inquiry into its accounting, AMD dropped 13 percent, Micron almost 14 percent, and CoreWeave and Nebius more than 10 percent each.

Anthropic's updated Form S-1 showed revenue of $33.2 billion through September 30 against $2.5 billion a year earlier, alongside roughly $900.9 billion in infrastructure and computing commitments running through 2045. The first nine months also brought a $104 billion net loss, the bulk of it non-cash charges tied to convertible notes, against an operating loss of $1.26 billion. That matters for who can own the stock: S&P 500 entry requires 12 months of trading and positive GAAP earnings, rules S&P Dow Jones Indices said in June it would keep for megacap listings, putting late 2027 at the earliest. Only about 6 percent of the company was sold, and the Nasdaq-100 is expected to add it at December's rebalancing. UBS's Ulrike Hoffmann-Burchardi said the debate has shifted from whether AI investment will continue to whether it can generate enough revenue and shareholder return, while Capital Economics' James Reilly said the falls have further to run.

OpenAI released GPT-6.2 Astra and GPT-6.2 Sol on November 17, cutting Astra's API rate by 20%, to $8 per million input tokens and $40 on output, with cached input down 60% to $0.40. Sol holds its predecessor's $2 and $10. Both models carry a 1.05 million-token context window, and requests above 272,000 input tokens move to a higher tier. OpenAI's own charts show Astra beating GPT-6 Astra on all nine head-to-head tests, including Agents' Last Exam at 78.3% against 73.1%.

Power users who tested the models before release reacted harshly, as VentureBeat's Carl Franzen reported. Investor Matt Shumer said he would skip his customary written review because the models "were essentially unusable." Developer Theo Browne called Astra the worst model OpenAI has ever released, writing that on a TypeScript compiler rewrite in Rust it spent seven days without landing an improvement and produced a version 56% slower. OpenAI Codex leader Thibault Sottiaux responded on X that the models changed substantially in the final three to four days before launch. OpenAI's system card reported that Astra hid or misreported deliberately inserted obstacles 22.28% of the time, roughly double GPT-6 Astra's 11.09%.

Google shipped four models over the same stretch. Gemini 4 Hydrogen arrived on November 12 as a faster but pricier successor to Flash. Gemini 4 Carbon, which [#347](https://lastweekin.ai/p/347) covered when it was limited to selected cyber defenders, became publicly available on November 10 at an introductory $2 and $10 per million tokens, rising to $4 and $20 after November 30. Gemini 4 Neon followed on November 18 at $3 and $15, which Google places 25% below Opus 5.5's standard rates while scoring within about a point of it on two engineering benchmarks. A fourth model, Gemini Decision 1, opened on November 11 and answers up to 1,000 questions about one set of material in a single call, at $0.10 per million input tokens with free output.

Anthropic had opened the run on November 9 with Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2, both priced at $8 and $40 per million tokens, 20% below the 5.1 generation. Microsoft used its Ignite conference on November 17 to announce its own batch, including MAI Code 2 in GitHub Copilot and MAI Cyber 2 Flash for verified security defenders, and said its Maia 200 chip has entered production. Alibaba's Qwen4-Max, announced the same day, charges $1.50 and $4.50 per million tokens, with Alibaba reporting token consumption down about 24% at its highest reasoning setting on the Work360 suite. The comparative pricing and benchmark claims in these launches come from the vendors themselves.

Nvidia announced on November 12 that Jensen Huang had resigned as president, CEO and board member, effective immediately, ending a 33-year run at the company he co-founded. CFO Colette Kress was named interim CEO while continuing as finance chief, and the board appointed Harvey C. Jones, former CEO of Synopsys, as its first-ever chair. The company's statement called Huang's contributions profound and immeasurable but gave no reason for his departure.

The sequence that led there — [#347](https://lastweekin.ai/p/347) covered the board investigation as it opened — was reported largely by The New York Times, which said it drew on a year of reporting and more than 100 people:

- In October, the Times reported that Huang had a close relationship with Alice Huang (no relation), an executive at Singapore-based data center firm Megaspeed, which had bought more than $2 billion of Nvidia technology in a year. He said the relationship was purely business.
- On November 6, the Times reported a classified memo drafted by Secretary of State Marco Rubio describing US surveillance that concluded she was passing information to Chinese intelligence; the Financial Times reported that night that the government believed the relationship was romantic.
- On November 8, Huang wrote to staff: "I was unfaithful to my wife... There is no excuse for what I did. I am deeply sorry."
- Days later, the Times contacted Nvidia about a second affair, with Kimberly Powell, Nvidia's vice president of healthcare, who reported directly to him. The board convened an emergency meeting, and Huang resigned the next morning.

Nvidia's first results without Huang, reported after the bell on November 17, beat expectations. Revenue reached $115.84 billion for the quarter ended October 25, more than double a year earlier, against the $109.10 billion analysts polled by LSEG had expected, with data center revenue of $107.6 billion. Kress guided to about $130 billion for the current quarter, above the $125.1 billion consensus, raised the fiscal 2028 growth forecast to 75% from 70%, and assumed no data center compute revenue from China at all. She said the company would be disciplined in committing capital and would rigorously evaluate future investments, commitments and guarantees, of which it disclosed $118 billion, mostly tied to an OpenAI project in Ohio.

Regulatory pressure has widened since. The Wall Street Journal reported an SEC civil investigation into whether Nvidia's disclosures misled investors, and Reuters reported on November 20 that enforcement staff are examining potential accounting issues, without identifying which practices or when the inquiry began. California attorney general Rob Bonta issued an investigative subpoena on November 19 over whether Nvidia or Huang's actions led to China obtaining advanced chips or confidential information, and whether investors were misled. Commerce and Justice have confirmed a criminal investigation into Megaspeed, a shareholder class action is pending in San Francisco, and Huang and general counsel Tim Teter are due before House committees on December 1 and 2. Bloomberg separately reported court filings from Nvidia's first in-house lawyer alleging Huang routinely berated employees; Trump said he would like Huang to get a second chance, and the board says it will not reinstate him.

#### Trump and Xi agree humans must stay in control of military AI as US chip rules tighten

 * [China's Xi pushes for global AI body at APEC in counter to US](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/chinas-xi-pushes-global-ai-045049885.html)
 * [Trump Administration Divide on China Policy Breaks Into Public View](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/16/us/politics/trump-china-ai-chip-policy.html)
 * [As Trump Goes to China, A.I. Tensions Surge Amid Spying and Safety Fears](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/17/us/politics/trump-china-ai-tensions.html)

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said on November 20 that Trump and Xi Jinping had agreed humans must always be involved in military decisions taken by advanced AI, which the administration calls superintelligence. Speaking to CNBC after returning from their meeting in Shenzhen on November 19, held alongside the APEC leaders' summit, he said such systems should not be allowed to act autonomously in major military actions. No major operation, he said, should be launched without a human involved. Bessent called it one of the trip's main achievements. Neither side published any text, so the understanding appears to be verbal rather than a signed commitment, and CNBC described the wider summit as yielding few deliverables.

On chips, Bessent said policy is unchanged: Nvidia's H200 remains the most advanced processor China can buy under license, and he said he would not anticipate any change. He described a paucity of Chinese orders for the H200 and put China's own capability many years behind, though Nvidia has in fact stopped shipping the part there. He also said remote access had been curtailed, pointing to the interim rule the Commerce Department issued on November 16.

That rule requires a license to send AI chips to data centers anywhere that might supply compute to companies based in China or the other embargoed countries on the US D:5 list, and it covers brokers and intermediaries. It applies to any site able to deliver more than 12 million total processing performance, roughly 750 H100 or H200 chips, and adds license requirements for Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Thailand and Brazil, which Commerce said carry a higher-than-normal risk of diversion. Existing operators have 90 days to obtain licenses, and the rule rescinds the Biden administration's AI Diffusion Rule from January 2025.

The House Foreign Affairs Committee voted on November 19 to release license records showing Commerce had approved sales of more than 879,000 AI chips to Chinese companies as of May 2026, from 3.4 million requested between December and May. Named buyers include Alibaba, Tencent, ByteDance, Baidu and Tesla's Shanghai operation, and some approvals covered firms the Pentagon designates as Chinese military companies; chair Brian Mast called two applicants, Maginfra and New Matrix, fake companies. Bernstein's Stacy Rasgon told CNBC the total was "not that many," about one approval in four. The two governments remain far apart on governance, with Trump rejecting global AI regulation at the summit while Xi called for an international AI body, and they are due to meet again at the G20 in Miami in December.
